## Bodies, shapes, and layers

Godot's physics moves **bodies**, and each body has one or more **collision shapes** that say how much space it takes. The shape is invisible and simple, such as a capsule or a box, because physics checks it many times per second; what you see is a separate [mesh](/reference/glossary/#mesh). Godot has three bodies you meet in this course:

- A `StaticBody3D` never moves: the ground, rocks, and walls.
- A `CharacterBody3D` moves only when your code tells it to, and slides along whatever it bumps into. It is made for players and characters.
- A `RigidBody3D` is moved by forces and gravity, like a ball. The template does not need one.

Each body sits on [physics layers](/reference/glossary/#physics-layer) and has a **mask**: the layers it collides with. In lesson 3 you named layer 1 `world` and layer 2 `player`. The player sits on `player` and collides with `world`.

## Write the movement, piece by piece

### Piece 1: walk

A `CharacterBody3D` has a `Velocity`: a `Vector3`, three numbers for the speed along x, y, and z, in meters per second. You set it, then call `MoveAndSlide()`, which moves the body by its velocity for one physics tick and slides it along anything it hits.

Movement belongs in `_PhysicsProcess(double delta)`, which Godot calls once per physics tick, 60 times a second. That keeps it in step with physics, and it is what physics interpolation from lesson 3 needs to draw it smoothly.

Change the template so that:

- `using System;` and the comments are gone, and `namespace TopDownTemplate.Player;` is under `using Godot;`: the project's name and the folder under `src`, as in lesson 5.
- Four `private static readonly StringName` fields, written as in lesson 5, hold the move actions: `MoveLeft` for `"move_left"`, `MoveRight` for `"move_right"`, `MoveForward` for `"move_forward"`, and `MoveBack` for `"move_back"`.
- An exported `float` property `Speed` defaults to `3.5f`, in meters per second, a light jog.
- `_Ready()` and `_Process()` are replaced by `public override void _PhysicsProcess(double delta)`, which:
	- reads the input with `var input = Input.GetVector(MoveLeft, MoveRight, MoveForward, MoveBack);`
	- sets `Velocity` to `new Vector3(input.X, 0f, input.Y) * Speed`
	- calls `MoveAndSlide();`

<Aside type="note" title="How four actions become one direction">
`Input.GetVector` combines four actions into a `Vector2`, two numbers from -1 to 1: `X` is left (-1) to right (1), and `Y` is forward (-1) to back (1). Diagonals are kept to a length of 1, so walking diagonally is not faster, and a stick pushed part of the way gives a shorter vector, so the player walks slower. Past the 0.2 deadzone, the range is stretched to fill 0 to 1, so a stick pushed halfway gives about 0.4. The game's ground is the x and z of the world, and the camera looks toward **-Z**, so the input's `Y` becomes the world's z: pushing forward walks away from the camera. `var` lets C# work out the type, here `Vector2`, from the right side.
</Aside>

### Piece 2: speed up, slow down, and fall

Change the script so that:

- A fifth field, `Sprint`, holds `"sprint"`, and two more exported `float` properties hold `SprintSpeed` (`6f`) and `Acceleration` (`30f`). Add a comment above `Acceleration` saying what it is: how quickly the player speeds up and slows down, in meters per second, per second.
- `_PhysicsProcess` first turns `delta`, the seconds since the last tick, into a `float`: `var step = (float)delta;`. It picks the speed with `var speed = Input.IsActionPressed(Sprint) ? SprintSpeed : Speed;` (the `? :` picks the first value when the condition is true and the second when it is false), and works out the velocity it wants: `var target = new Vector3(input.X, 0f, input.Y) * speed;`.
- Piece 1's line `Velocity = new Vector3(input.X, 0f, input.Y) * Speed;` is gone: the velocity now changes step by step instead of jumping to full speed.
- It copies `Velocity` into a variable: `var velocity = Velocity;`.
- It moves only the flat part of the velocity, x and z, toward `target`, and leaves y alone, because y is falling, which gravity handles below. Make the flat part with `new Vector3(velocity.X, 0f, velocity.Z)`, and call `MoveToward` on it: `a.MoveToward(b, most)` returns `a` moved toward `b` by at most `most`. Here `b` is `target` and `most` is `Acceleration * step`. Keep the result in a variable `horizontal`, then copy its two numbers back: `velocity.X = horizontal.X;` and `velocity.Z = horizontal.Z;`.
- When the body is not on the floor (`!IsOnFloor()`), it adds gravity: `velocity += GetGravity() * step;`. `GetGravity()` returns the project's gravity, 9.8 meters per second, per second, pointing down.
- It sets `Velocity = velocity;` and calls `MoveAndSlide();`.

<Aside type="note" title="Why copy Velocity first">
`Velocity` is a property holding a `Vector3`, and a `Vector3` is copied whenever you read it. `Velocity.X = 1f;` would change a copy and is not allowed, so you change your own copy and write it back. Moving toward the target by `Acceleration * step` each tick means the player reaches full speed in about a tenth of a second, a short ramp that feels responsive but not robotic.
</Aside>

<Aside type="note" title="Why reset the interpolation">
Physics interpolation draws a node partway between where it was on the last tick and where it is now. When the rig jumps to the player at the start, the jump would be drawn as a slide across the level in the first frame. `ResetPhysicsInterpolation()` tells Godot the jump is a teleport. The rig follows in `_PhysicsProcess`, the same step the player moves in, so both are drawn smoothly. The rig sits above the player in the Scene dock, so Godot runs it first and it copies where the player was one tick earlier, a sixtieth of a second behind: too little to see.
</Aside>

## If it does not work

- **The player falls through the ground at once.** The ground has no collision: check that `Ground` has a `StaticBody3D` with a `CollisionShape3D` under it. Or the player's **Mask** does not include layer 1.
- **The player never falls off the edge, or falls very slowly.** Piece 1's `Velocity = ...` line is still there, setting y back to 0 every tick, or the `MoveToward` line moves the whole velocity, y included, which pulls the fall back toward 0. Move only the flat part, `new Vector3(velocity.X, 0f, velocity.Z)`, and copy back only its X and Z.
- **`CS0120` or `CS1501` on the `MoveToward` line.** It was written `Vector3.MoveToward(a, b, most)`. In Godot's C#, `MoveToward` is called on a vector: `a.MoveToward(b, most)`.
- **The player sinks halfway into the ground.** The CollisionShape3D's **Position y** is not `0.9`.
- **The player walks backward compared to the keys, or sideways.** Check the order in `Input.GetVector(MoveLeft, MoveRight, MoveForward, MoveBack)`, and that `input.Y` goes into the z of the `Vector3`, not its y.
- **The capsule turns its back, not its nose, toward where it walks.** The last argument of `LookAt` must be `true`, and the nose must sit at **+Z** (position z `0.3`).
- **The Debugger shows `Node origin and target are in the same position, look_at() failed.`** `LookAt` was called while standing still, with no direction to face. Call it only when `target` is not `Vector3.Zero`.
- **The camera does not follow.** `Target` is not assigned on the meadow's `CameraRig`, or the project was not built after piece 4.
- **Keys do nothing.** Click inside the game first, so it has the keyboard.
- **`CS0103: The name 'Sprint' does not exist`.** The `Sprint` field is missing or misspelled.
