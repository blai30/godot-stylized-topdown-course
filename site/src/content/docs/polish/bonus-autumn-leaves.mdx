## What you will build

A grove where each oak has its own amount of autumn: some still green, some turning yellow-olive, some fully red and orange. You change no model and paint no new texture. The leaf atlas you painted in lesson 25 is a gray picture, and a shader decides what colors those grays become.

## Colors from grays

The foliage shader from lesson 27 already does one recoloring. The atlas is gray, near the middle, and the shader multiplies it by `leaf_color`, so the whole crown is green. Artists have several names for the ideas around this, and you will meet them in other engines' tutorials:

- A **tint mask** or **grayscale texture** is a picture whose job is only to say how light or dark each spot is. The color is added later.
- A **gradient map**, also called a **color ramp lookup** or a **palette remap**, is how the color is added: the gray at each pixel picks a color from a strip of colors. Dark grays pick colors from the strip's left end, light grays from its right end.
- A **flat multiply**, which the shader does today, scales every pixel's red, green, and blue by the same three numbers.

The difference matters for autumn. Try the flat way first.

## The flat multiply, and its limit

<Steps>

1. Open `art/nature/tree_oak/tree_oak.tscn` and select the `leaves` node.

2. In the Inspector, under **Geometry**, click the picture of **Material Override**. The Inspector now shows `leaves.tres`. Set **Leaf Color** to `#E6731F`, an orange, and save the scene with <Kbd keys="Ctrl+S" mac="Cmd+S" />.

3. Open `levels/meadow.tscn`, click **Play Scene**, <Kbd keys="F6" mac="Cmd+R" />, and zoom in on the oaks.

</Steps>

<Figure
	src={multiply}
	alt="The game at noon: the oaks have turned a flat, even orange, with the same bright top and dark underside the green ones had."
	caption="A flat multiply: orange crowns that are the green crowns with a different tint."
/>

It turned the leaves orange, and it is not bad. But look at what did not change. Every leaf is the same hue. The lit tops are lighter orange and the shaded leaves are darker orange, and that is all. Real autumn leaves shift hue as they get lighter: the shaded ones are deep red-brown and the sunlit ones go yellow. A multiply cannot do that, because it keeps the ratio between red, green, and blue on every pixel. A gradient map can, because each gray gets its own color.

<Steps>

4. Set **Leaf Color** back to `#68C343` and save the scene.

</Steps>

## A shader with a gradient map

The new shader keeps the green path exactly as it was, so the pine, which has its own green, is untouched. It adds a second color for each pixel, from a ramp, and a per-tree number that blends between the two.

<Steps>

1. In the FileSystem dock, double-click `art/shaders/foliage.gdshader`. The Shader Editor opens.

2. Above the comment for `tree_phase`, add the ramp. Below the `tree_phase` line, add `autumn`:

	```glsl
	// The colors the leaves turn to in autumn, from the atlas's darkest gray to its lightest.
	uniform sampler2D autumn_ramp : source_color, filter_linear;
	// Set on each tree, so a grove does not sway in step.
	instance uniform float tree_phase = 0.0;
	// How far this tree has turned, from 0 (green) to 1 (autumn).
	instance uniform float autumn : hint_range(0.0, 1.0) = 0.0;
	```

	`instance uniform` is what `tree_phase` already is: a value each copy of the tree carries for itself. `source_color` tells Godot the ramp is made of colors, so it converts them correctly for lighting.

3. Above the comment for `CANOPY_TOP`, add the range of grays the atlas actually uses:

	```glsl
	// The darkest and lightest grays the atlas uses, which the ramp is stretched across.
	const vec2 GRAY_RANGE = vec2(0.33, 0.72);
	```

	The atlas you painted keeps its grays between about 0.33 and 0.72; these are the values that fit the reference project's atlas. If the ramp were indexed by the raw gray, a third of it would never be used. This stretches the used grays across the whole ramp.

4. In `fragment()`, replace the line that starts `ALBEDO = leaf_color * leaf.rgb` with the lines below. Keep the `ALBEDO *= cloud_shadow` line from lesson 53 under it:

	```glsl
	vec3 green = leaf_color * leaf.rgb * LEAF_GAIN;
	// The leaf's gray picks a color from the ramp: darks turn deep red, lights turn yellow.
	float gray = clamp((leaf.r - GRAY_RANGE.x) / (GRAY_RANGE.y - GRAY_RANGE.x), 0.0, 1.0);
	vec3 turned = texture(autumn_ramp, vec2(gray, 0.5)).rgb;
	ALBEDO = mix(green, turned, autumn) * mix(CANOPY_UNDER, CANOPY_TOP, top) * inner;
	```

	`texture(autumn_ramp, vec2(gray, 0.5))` reads the ramp at the horizontal position `gray`. The ramp is one pixel tall, so its height does not matter. The canopy shading after it is unchanged: the autumn colors are lit and shaded like the green ones.

5. Save the shader with <Kbd keys="Ctrl+Alt+S" mac="Cmd+Option+S" />.

</Steps>

## Make the ramp

The ramp is a **GradientTexture1D**: a strip of pixels made from a **Gradient**, a list of color stops. You will make it on the oak's material.

<Steps>

1. Open `tree_oak.tscn`, select `leaves`, and click the picture of **Material Override** to open `leaves.tres`. A new row, **Autumn Ramp**, is at the bottom of **Shader Parameters**.

2. Click the **Autumn Ramp** field's empty value, choose **New GradientTexture1D**, and click the new texture's picture to open it. Click the **Gradient** field's picture in the same way. The Inspector shows a small black-to-white gradient with a stop at each end.

	<Figure
		src={rampDefault}
		alt="The Inspector showing the Autumn Ramp row, a GradientTexture1D, and below its Gradient field the gradient editor: a black to white bar with a stop at each end."
		caption="A new gradient: black at the left stop, white at the right."
	/>

3. Click the bar twice to add two stops, about 45 percent and 75 percent of the way across. Then select each stop in turn, click the color swatch at the right of the bar, and type its color in the picker's hex field:

	| Stop | Where | Color | What it is |
	|---|---|---|---|
	| 1 | 0 percent | `6B1F14` | The darkest leaves, a deep red-brown |
	| 2 | 45 percent | `CC4D1A` | The shaded leaves, a red-orange |
	| 3 | 75 percent | `EE8C1F` | The middle leaves, orange |
	| 4 | 100 percent | `FACC47` | The lit leaves, a warm yellow |

	The positions do not have to be exact. The offsets in the reference project came out at 0.447 and 0.745.

	<Figure
		src={ramp}

4. Save the scene with <Kbd keys="Ctrl+S" mac="Cmd+S" />. It saves the changes to `leaves.tres` too, with the gradient inside it.

</Steps>

## A script that sets each tree's autumn

Setting an instance shader parameter by hand on a copy of a scene is awkward, because the meshes are inside the instanced scene. Lesson 28's `WindPhase` already sets one parameter from a script on the tree's root. A script can also publish a setting in the Inspector, with `[Export]`, so each copy of the tree gets its own value there.

<Steps>

1. In your editor, create `src/World/AutumnTree.cs` and write it from the list below. The finished script is in the solution under the list.

2. Click **Build Project** in Godot's toolbar.

</Steps>

Write the script so that:

- it has `using System.Linq;` and `using Godot;`, and is in the namespace `TopDownTemplate.World;`;
- a comment says it is a tree that also has its own place in the season, set in the Inspector, where 0 is green and 1 is autumn. `AutumnTree` is a `partial class` that inherits **`WindPhase`**, not `Node3D`: it is the same tree with one more setting, and it keeps lesson 28's phase for free;
- a `private static readonly StringName` named `AutumnParameter` holds `"autumn"`;
- a public `float` property `Autumn` with `{ get; set; }`, marked `[Export(PropertyHint.Range, "0,1,0.01")]`, which gives the Inspector a slider from 0 to 1;
- `_Ready()` is an `override` that first calls `base._Ready()`, so the wind phase is set as before. Then it loops over `GetChildren().OfType<GeometryInstance3D>()` and calls `SetInstanceShaderParameter(AutumnParameter, Autumn)` on each.

<Aside type="tip" title="A whole season">
A tree with its own `autumn` is a grove of different trees. To have the whole meadow turn together, add a global shader parameter named `season` as you did `wind_strength` in lesson 27, read it in the shader with something like `clamp(season * 2.0 - tree_phase, 0.0, 1.0)` in place of `autumn`, and let a script move it from 0 to 1 over the year. The trees would turn one after the other, in the order of their wind phases, so the meadow changes gradually instead of all at once.
</Aside>

## If it does not work

- **The Autumn Ramp row is not in the material.** The shader was not saved, or it has an error. Check the Shader Editor's bottom panel, and that the line reads `uniform sampler2D autumn_ramp : source_color, filter_linear;`.
- **The oaks stay green at `1`.** The script is not on `TreeOak`, or the project was not built after you saved it. Click **Build Project**, and check that the Inspector shows **Autumn** at the top. Also check that the meadow's trees were saved after you set the values.
- **The oaks turn solid black or white.** The ramp is empty or not set: check **Autumn Ramp** in the material, and that its gradient has four stops.
- **The autumn colors look much too dark or too light.** `GRAY_RANGE` does not match your atlas. If you painted a lighter or darker atlas than the reference, change the two numbers to its darkest and lightest grays.
- **Godot says `AutumnTree` cannot be loaded.** The class name and the file name must match, and the namespace must be `TopDownTemplate.World`. Build again.
- **The oak stopped swaying.** `_Ready()` does not call `base._Ready()`, so the wind phase is never set.
