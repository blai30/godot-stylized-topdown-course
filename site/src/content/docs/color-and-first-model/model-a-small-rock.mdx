## What you will build

A small rock, about 60 cm across: a chunky shape with broad flat faces and soft rounded edges. It is the first model in your art library, and the technique carries over to every [hard-surface model](/reference/glossary/#hard-surface-model) in the course.

<Figure
	src={final}
	alt="A small gray rock in Blender's viewport: a squat, uneven block with a narrower top, broad flat faces, and softly rounded edges that catch the light."
	caption="The reference rock: 206 triangles, about 0.66 by 0.57 by 0.36 meters. Yours will have its own shape."
/>

## What makes a rock read from above

Your camera looks down at the world from high up, so players see a rock mostly from the top and from one side. Three things make it read as a rock at that distance:

- **An uneven silhouette from above.** A perfect box or circle looks man-made. Push a few corners in and out until the outline is irregular.
- **Broad, flat faces.** Big planes catch light in clear patches of bright and dark, which reads as solid stone. Lots of small bumps turn into noise at a distance.
- **Soft edges.** Real stone has worn edges. A narrow [bevel](/reference/glossary/#bevel) on each edge catches a thin line of light and makes the shape feel heavy and real.

The [triangle budget](/reference/glossary/#triangle-budget) for a small rock is **100 to 400 triangles**. That is plenty, because the shape does the work, not the detail.

<Aside type="tip" title="Two habits for Blender hotkeys">
- Blender hotkeys act on the area under the mouse pointer. Keep the pointer over the 3D viewport when you press them.
- No number pad on your keyboard? Wherever this lesson says <Kbd keys="Numpad 1" /> or <Kbd keys="Numpad 7" />, use **View > Viewpoint > Front** or **View > Viewpoint > Top** in the viewport's header, or the <Kbd keys="`" /> pie menu, as lesson 8 showed.
</Aside>

## Start from the cube

<Steps>

1. Choose **File > New > General**. If Blender asks **Save changes before closing?**, click **Don't Save**. The new scene is your startup file from lesson 8: one cube, already selected, with the statistics in the top left corner. The cube is your starting block.

	<Figure

		alt="Blender's 3D viewport with a single cube selected, outlined in orange, and the statistics in the top left: Objects 1/1, Vertices 8/8, Edges 12/12, Faces 6/6, Triangles 12/12."
		caption="The cube from your startup file is 2 meters on each side."
	/>

2. Name it: with the pointer over the 3D view, press <Kbd keys="F2" />. An **Object Name** box opens. Press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+A" mac="Cmd+A" /> to select the old name, type `rock_small`, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />. Every object in the course is named after what it is, in lowercase with underscores, so you can find it later in Godot.

	<Figure
		src={rename}
		alt="A small box titled Object Name with the text rock_small in its field."
		caption="F2 renames the selected object."
	/>

3. Save right away: choose **File > Save As** (<Kbd keys="Ctrl+Shift+S" mac="Cmd+Shift+S" />). Blender opens its **File Browser** in a window of its own.

4. Click the path field at the top of the File Browser, press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+A" mac="Cmd+A" /> to select the path in it, and type the path to a new `nature\rock_small` folder in your project's `art_source` folder, ending with a backslash. If your project is in `Documents\top-down-template`, that is `C:\Users\<your name>\Documents\top-down-template\art_source\nature\rock_small\`. Press <Kbd keys="Enter" />.

	<Figure

		caption="The path field, with the folder you want typed in. Your path starts with your own project's location."

5. The folders do not exist yet, so Blender asks **Create new directory?** Click **Create**. Blender makes both folders and opens the new one.

	<Figure
		src={createFolder}
		alt="A small box asking Create new directory? with Create and Cancel buttons."
		caption="Blender creates every missing folder in the path at once."
	/>

6. Click the file name field at the bottom, press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+A" mac="Cmd+A" />, type `rock_small.blend`, press <Kbd keys="Enter" />, and click **Save As**.

	<Figure
		src={saveName}

<Aside type="note" title="Why art_source">
Your `.blend` and Krita files live in `art_source`, outside the folders Godot imports, because Godot cannot read them. In lesson 10 you export a file Godot can read into the `art` folder. Keeping sources apart keeps the project tidy, and lesson 2 told Godot to skip that folder entirely. Each time you save again, Blender keeps the previous version beside it as `rock_small.blend1`; lesson 2's `.gitignore` keeps those backups out of Git.
</Aside>

## Block out the shape

<Steps>

1. With `rock_small` selected and the pointer over the 3D view, press <Kbd keys="N" /> to open the **Sidebar**. On the **Item** tab, under **Dimensions**, click the **X** field, press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+A" mac="Cmd+A" />, type `0.6`, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />. Do the same with `0.5` for **Y** and `0.35` for **Z**. Blender reads plain numbers as meters.

	<Figure
		src={dimensions}
		alt="The Sidebar's Item tab: Location 0 m, Rotation 0 degrees, Scale X 0.300, Y 0.250, Z 0.175, and Dimensions X 0.6 m, Y 0.5 m, Z 0.35 m."
		caption="Typing the dimensions stretches the object: its Scale changes, not its mesh."
	/>

2. Apply that size so it becomes the [mesh](/reference/glossary/#mesh)'s real size: move the pointer off the Sidebar onto the empty part of the 3D view, press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+A" />, and choose **Scale** in the **Apply** menu. The **Scale** fields return to `1.000`.

	<Figure
		src={applyMenu}
		alt="The Apply menu: Location, Rotation, Scale, All Transforms, Rotation & Scale, then the Deltas options and more."
		caption="Ctrl+A opens the Apply menu."
	/>

	<Aside type="note" title="Why apply the scale">
	Before applying, the mesh is still a 2-meter cube that the object stretches to the size you typed. Modifiers such as Bevel measure in the mesh's own size, so on a stretched object a 4 cm bevel would come out a different width on every side. Applying the scale bakes the size into the mesh, so every later measurement is true.
	</Aside>

3. Press <Kbd keys="N" /> to close the Sidebar, then <Kbd keys="Numpad ." /> to zoom in on the rock (without a number pad: **View > Frame Selected**). Press <Kbd keys="Tab" /> to enter [Edit Mode](/reference/glossary/#edit-mode), where you change the mesh itself.

4. Add a [loop cut](/reference/glossary/#loop-cut) around the middle: point at one of the vertical edges, press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+R" />, and a yellow line circles the rock across that edge. Click once, then right-click. The right-click cancels the sliding part, so the cut stays centered.

	<Figure
		src={loopCut}
		alt="The rock in Edit Mode seen up close, with a yellow loop around its middle showing where the cut will go."
		caption="The yellow preview runs across the edge under the pointer."
	/>

5. Repeat the loop cut twice more, pointing at edges that run in the other two directions, so the cube is cut once around each axis.

6. Press <Kbd keys="1" /> for [vertex](/reference/glossary/#vertex) selection, then <Kbd keys="Numpad 1" /> to look from the front and <Kbd keys="Alt+Z" /> to turn on [X-ray](/reference/glossary/#x-ray), so a box selection reaches the vertices on the back too.

7. Press <Kbd keys="Alt+A" /> to select nothing. Press <Kbd keys="B" />, then drag a box around the top row of vertices: press the left mouse button above and to the left of the row, move below and to the right of it, and release.

	<Figure
		src={boxSelect}
		alt="The rock from the front in X-ray, with a dashed box being dragged around the top edge."
		caption="B starts a box selection, which adds what is inside the box to the selection; Alt+A cleared it first."
	/>

8. Press <Kbd keys="S" />, type `0.75`, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />. The top is now narrower than the base, like a stone that has settled. Keep X-ray on.

	<Figure
		src={blocked}
		alt="The rock from the front in Edit Mode: a block whose top is narrower than its base, cut once around the middle in each direction, with the top row of vertices selected."
		caption="The [blockout](/reference/glossary/#blockout) after the loop cuts and the narrower top."
	/>

9. Now break the symmetry from above. Press <Kbd keys="Numpad 7" /> to look down from the top. The outline you see is the base and the ring around the middle: at each corner of the outline, a middle vertex sits right above a base vertex. For three or four corners: press <Kbd keys="Alt+A" />, press <Kbd keys="B" /> and drag a small box around the corner, which selects both vertices of that stack, then press <Kbd keys="G" />, move the mouse to push them out or pull them in by a few centimeters, and click. The outline should look irregular, not like a box. Then press <Kbd keys="Alt+Z" /> to turn X-ray off.

10. Add a little natural unevenness to everything: press <Kbd keys="A" /> to select all, then choose **Mesh > Transform > Randomize** in the viewport's header.

	<Figure
		src={transformMenu}
		alt="The Mesh menu with Transform open: Move, Rotate, Scale, To Sphere, Shear, Bend, Push/Pull, To Circle, Flatten, Shrink/Fatten, Space Edge Loops Evenly, Skin Resize, Warp, and Randomize at the bottom."
		caption="Randomize is the last entry in Mesh > Transform."
	/>

11. A **Randomize** panel appears at the bottom left of the viewport; if it shows only its title, click the title to open it. Click **Amount**, press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+A" mac="Cmd+A" />, type `0.03`, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />. **Random Seed** picks a different random pattern; try a few values until you like the result.

	<Figure
		src={randomizePanel}
		alt="The Randomize panel: Amount 0.03 m, Uniform 0.000, Normal 0.000, Random Seed 4."
		caption="Amount 0.03 m. The reference rock uses Random Seed 4."
	/>

	<Figure
		src={randomized}
		alt="The rock seen from the top in Edit Mode, all selected, after Randomize: an uneven outline, with no face perfectly square."
		caption="Randomize moves every vertex a small random amount. Keep it small; the big shapes should come from your own moves."
	/>

12. Set the rock on the ground. Look from the front (<Kbd keys="Numpad 1" />). Keep everything selected, press <Kbd keys="G" /> then <Kbd keys="Z" />, and move the mouse until the rock's lowest point touches the red line, which is the ground. Click to confirm. Instead of moving the mouse, you can type the distance, about `0.18`, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />; then check the lowest point against the red line and move again if it is off. A gap of a few millimeters is fine, because nobody sees it from the game camera.

	<Aside type="note" title="Why the base sits at zero">
	You moved the mesh, not the object, so the object's [origin](/reference/glossary/#origin) stayed at the center of the world, which is now the center of the rock's base. In Godot, a rock placed at any spot then sits on the ground there instead of sinking halfway into it.
	</Aside>

13. Press <Kbd keys="Tab" /> to go back to [Object Mode](/reference/glossary/#object-mode).

</Steps>

## Bevels and clean shading

<Steps>

1. Choose **Object > Shade Smooth** in the viewport's header. The rock now looks soft and blobby, with dark smudges across its big faces. That is expected; the next steps fix it. A **Shade Smooth** panel appears at the bottom left with **Keep Sharp Edges** checked; leave it.

	<Figure
		src={objectMenu}
		alt="The 3D view's Object menu open, with Shade Smooth, Shade Auto Smooth, and Shade Flat near the bottom."
		caption="Object > Shade Smooth."
	/>

	<Figure
		src={smoothOnly}
		alt="The rock from the front with smooth shading only: its faces look rounded and puffy, like clay, with soft dark gradients across what should be flat sides."
		caption="Smooth shading alone blends the lighting across the whole rock, so flat faces look curved."
	/>

	<Aside type="note" title="Why smooth shading first">
	[Smooth shading](/reference/glossary/#smooth-shading) makes lighting blend from face to face instead of jumping at every edge. On its own it makes flat faces look round. The bevel and the weighted normals below decide where the blend happens: only along the narrow edges, so the big faces stay flat and the edges look softly rounded.
	</Aside>

2. With `rock_small` still selected, go to the **Properties** editor at the bottom right and click the **Modifiers** tab (the blue wrench). A [modifier](/reference/glossary/#modifier) changes the mesh without destroying it. Click **Add Modifier** at the top of the tab, then **Generate > Bevel**.

	<Figure
		src={generateMenu}
		alt="The Add Modifier menu with Generate open to its left: Array, Array (Legacy), Bevel, Boolean, Build, and more."
		caption="Add Modifier > Generate > Bevel."
	/>

3. In the **Bevel** panel, click **Amount**, press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+A" mac="Cmd+A" />, type `0.04`, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />. Set **Segments** to `2` the same way. Leave **Limit Method** on **Angle**, at `30°`: only edges sharper than 30 degrees get a bevel, so the softer folds you made with Randomize stay as they are.

4. Click **Add Modifier** again. The menu opens with **Generate**, the last group you used, under the pointer. Move down to **Normals** and click **Weighted Normal**. In its panel, check **Keep Sharp**, so it leaves any edge marked sharp crisp; this rock has none yet, but it is a safe default. It must be below **Bevel** in the list, because modifiers run from top to bottom. If it ended up above, drag it down by the dotted grip at the right end of its title bar.

	<Figure
		src={normalsMenu}
		alt="The Add Modifier menu with Normals open: Normal Edit, Weighted Normal, and Smooth by Angle."
		caption="Add Modifier > Normals > Weighted Normal."
	/>

	<Figure
		src={modifierStack}
		alt="The Modifiers tab with two modifiers. Bevel: Edges, Width Type Offset, Amount 0.04 m, Segments 2, Limit Method Angle, Angle 30 degrees. Below it, WeightedNormal: Weighting Mode Face Area, Weight 50, Threshold 0.01, Keep Sharp checked."
		caption="The finished modifier stack: Bevel first, then Weighted Normal."
	/>

	<Aside type="note" title="Why weighted normals">
	A [normal](/reference/glossary/#normal) is the direction a surface faces, and lighting depends on it. The Weighted Normal modifier points each vertex's normal mostly toward the biggest face beside it, so large faces light as flat planes and the small bevel faces carry all the rounding. That is the look of [weighted normals](/reference/glossary/#weighted-normals): crisp planes, soft edges, and no smudges.
	</Aside>

</Steps>

## Check the triangle count and origin

<Steps>

1. Read the statistics in the top left of the viewport, which you turned on in your startup file. Make sure **Triangles** is between 100 and 400. The count includes the bevels, because Blender shows the result of the modifiers.

	<Figure
		src={statistics}
		alt="The statistics overlay in the top left of the viewport: Objects 1/1, Vertices 105/105, Edges 206/206, Faces 103/103, Triangles 206/206."
		caption="The reference rock has 206 triangles, well inside the 100 to 400 budget."
	/>

2. Look from the front (<Kbd keys="Numpad 1" />). The small orange dot, the origin, should sit at the middle of the rock's base. The red and white ring at the same spot is the **3D cursor**, a marker for where new objects appear; it is not part of the rock.

	<Figure
		src={frontOrigin}
		alt="A front view of the rock in orthographic mode: the rock rests on the red ground line, and its origin sits at the center of its base."
		caption="From the front: the rock rests on the ground line, with its origin at the center of its base."
	/>

3. Press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+S" mac="Cmd+S" /> to save.

</Steps>

## Try it

Orbit around the rock with the middle mouse button and look at it from above with <Kbd keys="Numpad 7" />. You should see:

- An irregular outline from above, not a box.
- Big faces that look flat, with a thin bright line along each edge where the bevel catches the light.
- No dark smudges across the faces.

## If it does not work

- **The bevel is much wider on some sides than others.** The scale was not applied. Select the rock, press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+A" />, choose **Scale**, and the bevel evens out.
- **Dark smudges remain across the faces.** The **Weighted Normal** modifier is missing, or is above **Bevel** in the stack.
- **The loop cut slid off-center.** You moved the mouse before right-clicking. Press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+Z" mac="Cmd+Z" /> and cut again: click once, then right-click right away.
- **No yellow line appears after Ctrl+R.** The pointer was not over an edge of the rock, or you were in Object Mode. Press <Kbd keys="Tab" /> for Edit Mode and point at an edge.
- **Scaling also moved vertices you did not want.** Something else was still selected when you drew the box, because B adds to the selection. Undo, press <Kbd keys="Alt+A" />, and select again.
- **You can only select the vertices in front.** X-ray is off. Press <Kbd keys="Alt+Z" />.
- **A typed number was added to the old one, such as `20.6`.** The old value was not selected. Click the field, press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+A" mac="Cmd+A" />, and type again.
- **The triangle count is over 400.** Check that **Segments** is `2`, not higher, and that you made three loop cuts, not more.
- **Blender ignores a hotkey or does something else.** The mouse pointer was not over the 3D viewport. Move it over the viewport and press the key again.
- **The rock floats above or sinks below the red line.** In Edit Mode, select all and move it with <Kbd keys="G" /> then <Kbd keys="Z" /> until the lowest point touches the line.
