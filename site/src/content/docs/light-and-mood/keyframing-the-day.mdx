## The idea: a color grade

Look at a painting of dusk: the shadows are purple and the highlights orange, and the green of the grass is hardly green at all. A light can warm the lit side of a rock, but it cannot do that to shadows, because shadows are lit by the sky. A **color grade** works on the finished picture instead: it shifts the dark pixels toward one tint, the bright pixels toward another, and changes how colorful everything is. It is the same step a film colorist does.

Godot can apply a grade in its tonemapping, through a 3D look-up table. The template uses a small shader that you can read, change, and animate. It is a canvas shader on a `ColorRect` that covers the screen, and it reads the frame Godot has drawn, after tonemapping, through `hint_screen_texture`.

The formula is FactoryGame's. For each pixel of color `c` with brightness `L`:

`graded = L + (c - L) × saturation + (1 - L)² × shadow chroma + L² × highlight chroma`

The first part changes saturation around the pixel's gray. The shadow tint counts most for dark pixels, because `(1 - L)²` is large when `L` is small. The highlight tint counts most for bright pixels. A tint's **chroma** is the tint less its own gray, so a white tint adds nothing, and the grade can shift color without changing brightness. A **strength** from 0 to 1 mixes the graded pixel with the original.

Replace the shader's contents so that:

- it starts with `shader_type canvas_item;`;
- `screen_texture` is a `sampler2D` with `hint_screen_texture` and `filter_nearest`, with a comment: the frame Godot has drawn, after tonemapping. `hint_screen_texture` makes Godot fill the sampler with the screen behind this rectangle;
- `shadow_tint` and `highlight_tint` are `vec3` uniforms with `source_color`, both `vec3(1.0)`, with a comment above them: the tint lifted into the shadows and the tint gained in the highlights, where only their color counts and not their brightness;
- `strength` is a `float` with `hint_range(0.0, 1.0)`, defaulting to `0.0`, with a comment: how much of the grade shows, where 0 leaves the frame as it is;
- `saturation` is a `float` with `hint_range(0.0, 2.0)`, defaulting to `1.0`, with a comment: the graded color's saturation, where 1 keeps it;
- a function `float luma(vec3 color)` returns `dot(color, vec3(0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722))`, with a comment: how bright a color looks to the eye. The three numbers are how much the eye counts red, green, and blue;
- `fragment()` reads `vec3 color = texture(screen_texture, SCREEN_UV).rgb;` and `float light = luma(color);`;
- it makes `shadow_chroma` and `highlight_chroma`, each a tint less its own `luma`, with a comment that white has none;
- `graded` adds up the formula above, in one statement over three lines: `light + (color - light) * saturation`, then `(1.0 - light) * (1.0 - light) * shadow_chroma`, then `light * light * highlight_chroma`;
- it ends with `COLOR = vec4(clamp(mix(color, graded, strength), 0.0, 1.0), 1.0);`. `mix` takes the original at strength 0 and the graded color at 1, and `clamp` keeps each channel between 0 and 1.

<Aside type="note" title="Why after tonemapping">
The grade reads the frame after Godot has tone mapped it, so its colors are the ones the player sees, from 0 to 1, and a tint of 0.3 means 30 percent of a visible change. Before tonemapping the values are light energies with no top, and the same numbers would mean something different in every scene.
</Aside>

## The idea: an animation of the day

An **AnimationPlayer** holds animations, and an animation holds **tracks**. A property track names one property of one node or resource, such as `Sun:light_energy`, and holds **keyframes**: a time and a value. At any time, the player sets the property to the value between its two nearest keyframes. One player can drive the sun, the Environment, the sky shader's colors, and the grade at once, and one number, the time, picks the hour.

Your `day` animation is 60 seconds long. Noon is at 30 seconds, midnight at 0 and at 60, and everything between is keyed from FactoryGame's keyframe table.

## Key a track by hand

The easiest property to key is the ambient light, the energy of the sky that you set in lesson 19. Its values through the day, from FactoryGame: bright at night, dimmer by day.

| Time of day | Seconds | Ambient energy |
|---|---|---|
| Morning, held through noon | `16.2` | `0.65` |
| Midnight, and until pre-dawn | `0` and `7.2` | `1.5` |
| Sunrise and early morning | `9` and `12` | `1.0` |
| Afternoon | `43.8` | `0.65` |
| Late sunset | `48` | `1.1` |
| Sunset | `51` | `1.0` |
| Midnight again | `60` | `1.5` |

<Steps>

1. Select the root node and open the Environment in the Inspector, as in lesson 19. Switch on **Adjustments > On**, which the day's saturation needs later.

2. Type `16.2` into the time box at the top left of the Animation panel, the one that reads `0.0`. This is where the key will go.

3. In the Inspector, set **Background > Energy Multiplier** to `0.65`. Click the small key button at the right of its row. Godot asks **Create new track for property 'environment:background_energy_multiplier' and insert key?** Accept it.

	<Aside type="note" title="Key the daytime value first">

## The rest of the tracks, from a table

The day has twelve more tracks. Placing about a hundred and twenty keys by hand teaches nothing more than the first track did. A short tool script adds them from tables of values, as `GrassDetail` drew the grass in lesson 13.

Make a script `res://src/Tools/BuildDay.cs` that inherits `EditorScript`, as in lesson 13. Change it so that:

- it has `using Godot;`, the namespace `TopDownTemplate.Tools;`, and a comment above the class: it adds the day animation's tracks from the tables below, and you run it from the Script editor with **File > Run**. `[Tool]` is on the line above `public partial class BuildDay : EditorScript`;
- it has the constants `AnimationName` (`"day"`) and `DayLength` (`60.0`);
- three `readonly record struct` types describe a row of each table: `LightKey` has a `Time`, a `Color` as text, and an `Energy`; `DirectionKey` has a `Time` and a `Vector3` `Rotation`; and `LookKey` has a `Time`, the colors `Zenith`, `Horizon`, and `Fog`, the numbers `Saturation` and `Glow`, the grade's `ShadowTint` and `HighlightTint`, and its `GradeStrength` and `GradeSaturation`. A record struct is a small bundle of named values, with a constructor for free;
- three static arrays hold the tables: `Lights`, `Directions`, and `Looks`. Fill `Looks` from the table below, and copy `Lights` and `Directions` from the solution. Times are from 0 to 1 and noon is 0.5;
- `_Run()` gets the player with `EditorInterface.Singleton.GetEditedSceneRoot().GetNode<AnimationPlayer>("Day")` and its animation with `GetAnimation(AnimationName)`. It loops over each table and calls `Insert` for each property, using the track paths in the solution. It ends by marking the scene unsaved with `EditorInterface.Singleton.MarkSceneAsUnsaved()`, so Godot offers to save, and prints how many tracks the animation has;
- `Insert` finds the track for a path with `FindTrack`, adds it with `AddTrack` and `TrackSetPath` the first time, and puts a key at `time * DayLength` with `TrackInsertKey`.

The values are FactoryGame's Meridian keyframes, trimmed to what Godot animates directly. The script adds one more row at `1.00` that repeats midnight, so the day loops without a jump:

The sun's rows deserve a word. The same light is the sun from sunrise to sunset and the moon the rest of the time. The moon is a cyan-blue `#78B8FF` at energy `0.45`, pointing from the north-northwest. Its energy fades to 0 over the 4 percent of the day before sunrise, and the light turns into the sun *while the energy is 0*: the rows at `0.15` and `0.1501` (six thousandths of a second apart in the animation) hold the moon's color and direction, then the sun's, both at energy 0. Nothing is lit when the swap happens, so the player never sees it. Sunset does the same in reverse at `0.85` and `0.8501`. The shadows never switch off, as lesson 20 said, so this costs nothing.

The sun's rotation rows come from its path: it rises in the east at `0.15`, reaches 62 degrees above the horizon at noon, from the top left of the screen as in lesson 20, and sets in the west at `0.85`.

## If it does not work

- **The tracks show a red icon.** The path does not match a node or property. Track paths are relative to the AnimationPlayer's **Root Node**, which is the default `..`, the parent, here `WorldEnvironment`. Check that the nodes are named `Sun`, `Grade`, and `Screen`.
- **The sky does not change when you scrub.** The Environment's sky colors are shader parameters, and the track path is `.:environment:sky:sky_material:shader_parameter/zenith_color`. If you replaced the sky material since lesson 19, run `BuildDay` again.
- **The grade never shows.** Check that `Screen` covers the screen, is below `Grade`, and has its material set, and that **Strength** is above 0 in the tracks.
- **Everything looks gray at noon in the game.** The grade's **Saturation** is not `1.0`, or the Environment's **Adjustments** saturation is not `1.0` at noon.
- **The scene is saved at midnight.** You saved with the time box on `0`, so the properties took the midnight values. Set the time to `30` and save again.
- **BuildDay prints nothing.** The `Day` animation player does not exist, or the animation is not called `day`. Godot reports the error in the Output panel.
