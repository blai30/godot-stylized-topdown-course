## What you will build

A hand-painted grass texture: dense tufts of tapered blades, darker at the back and lit at the tips, in light and shadowed patches. It is [tileable](/reference/glossary/#tileable-texture): copies laid side by side join with no visible edge, so it can cover any amount of ground. In lesson 15 your ground shader repeats it across the meadow, and in a later module real 3D grass grows on top of it.

## A texture that repeats

Ground is too big for one picture. Instead, one small square image repeats across it like floor tiles. For that to work, whatever leaves the image at its right edge must come back in at its left edge, and the same at the top and bottom. Paint a stroke that stops at the edge, and every copy shows a hard line there, a **seam**.

Krita's **wrap-around mode** solves this while you paint. It shows copies of your image all around it, and a stroke that runs off one edge continues on the opposite edge. You paint on the copies as if they were one endless surface, and the seams never appear.

Your texture is 512 by 512 pixels and covers 2 by 2 meters of ground, so each meter gets 256 pixels. That number is the [texel density](/reference/glossary/#texel-density), and this course keeps it the same for every texture, so grass, dirt, and sand look equally sharp side by side.

## How hand-painted textures are made

Stylized games such as Breath of the Wild, Genshin Impact, and Tiny Glade paint their surfaces as clean shapes, not blurry noise. Each feature, here a tuft of grass, is drawn as a crisp shape with a darker side, a middle tone, and a lit edge, and features overlap back to front, so the ones in front look closer.

Painting thousands of grass blades one by one would take days. Texture artists paint a few good tufts by hand and turn them into a **stamp brush** that prints the tuft over and over, turned and sized a little differently each time. That is what you do here, in three parts: a brush that paints one tapered blade, three tufts painted with it, and a stamp brush made from your best tuft.

The colors are your palette's grass ramp from lesson 7. The reference values, as hue, saturation, and value, are below; use your own ramp in their place:

- **Shadow**: 104, 52, 38
- **Dark**: 96, 56, 51
- **Light**: 89, 56, 64
- **Highlight**: 82, 50, 77

## Create the image

<Steps>

1. Open Krita and choose **File > New...** (<Kbd keys="Ctrl+N" mac="Cmd+N" />). On the **Dimensions** tab, set **Width** and **Height** to `512` pixels.

	<Figure
		src={newDimensions}
		alt="Krita's Create new document dialog on the Dimensions tab: Width 512 and Height 512 in Pixels, Resolution 300 Pixels/Inch, Model RGB/Alpha, Depth 8-bit integer/channel, and an sRGB profile."
		caption="512 by 512 pixels: 2 meters of ground at 256 pixels per meter."
	/>

2. Click the **Content** tab, set **Name** to `grass_albedo`, and click **Create**. [Albedo](/reference/glossary/#albedo) is a surface's plain color; the name says what the texture is for.

3. Click the **foreground color** square in the toolbar, as in lesson 7. On the **HSV** tab, type the **shadow** swatch, `104`, `52`, `38`, pressing <Kbd keys="Tab" /> after each, and click **OK**.

	<Figure
		src={colorShadow}
		alt="The Select a Color dialog on its HSV tab: Hue 104 degrees, Saturation 52%, Value 38%, a dark green in the patch at the bottom left."
		caption="The shadow green: whatever shows between the tufts."
	/>

4. Choose **Edit > Fill with Foreground Color** (<Kbd keys="Shift+Backspace" />). With nothing selected, Krita fills the whole layer, **Paint Layer 1**.

5. Choose **View > Wrap Around Mode** (<Kbd keys="Shift+W" />), then zoom out a step with <Kbd keys="Ctrl+-" mac="Cmd+-" />. Krita now draws copies of the image all around it; while the image is one flat color, you cannot see them yet.

</Steps>

## Make a blade brush

A pen tablet makes a stroke thin where you press lightly. A mouse presses evenly, so its strokes are the same width from end to end. Krita's brushes can taper by another rule instead: **Fade** shrinks the brush as the stroke gets longer.

<Steps>

1. Press <Kbd keys="B" /> for the **Freehand Brush Tool**.

2. In the **Brush Presets** docker at the bottom right, click the **Search** box at the bottom and type `Basic-5 Size`. Two brushes are left; hold the pointer over each to see its name, and click **b) Basic-5 Size**, not **Basic-5 Size Opacity**.

	<Figure
		src={presetSearch}
		alt="The Brush Presets docker with a search typed into its Search box at the bottom and the matching brushes in the list."
		caption="Search narrows the list."
	/>

3. Press <Kbd keys="F5" /> to open the **Brush Editor**. At its bottom, uncheck **Temporarily Save Tweaks To Presets**. With it checked, Krita remembers every change you make to a built-in brush until you close Krita, so the next time you pick that brush it is not the one you expect. Unchecked, your changes live only in the brushes you save. Krita remembers this setting.

4. The editor's middle column lists the brush's options. Click **Size** in that list. In the list of inputs that appears, check **Fade**, then uncheck **Pressure**. (Krita keeps at least one input checked, so check the new one first.)

5. Click the word **Fade** to show its settings below the list. Right-click the **Length** field, type `45`, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />.

6. Above the curve, click the second button, the line falling from top left to bottom right. The curve now starts at 100% and falls to 0%: the brush starts at full size and shrinks to a point by the end of the length.

	<Figure
		src={bladeSettings}
		alt="The Brush Editor showing the Size option: Fade checked in the inputs list and Pressure unchecked, Length 45 below the list, and a curve falling in a straight line from 100% at the top left to 0% at the bottom right."
		caption="Size driven by Fade: full size at the start of a stroke, a point after 45."
	/>

7. Click **Save New Brush Preset...** at the top right of the editor. Set the name to `Grass Blade` and click **Save**. Your brush now has its own entry in the Brush Presets docker. Press <Kbd keys="F5" /> again to close the editor.

	<Figure
		src={savePreset}
		alt="The Save New Brush Preset dialog with the name Grass Blade typed in and a preview of the brush stroke."
		caption="A brush of your own, saved with your Krita settings."
	/>

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip" title="Typing into Krita's slider fields">
Fields such as **Size**, **Opacity**, and **Length** are sliders: a left click or drag changes the value by where you click. To type an exact number, right-click the field, type, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />.
</Aside>

## Paint three tufts

You paint the tufts in grays, not green. The stamp brush you make next keeps their light and dark but takes its color from the foreground color, so one tuft can print in every green of your ramp.

<Steps>

1. Choose **Layer > New > Add Paint Layer** (<Kbd keys="Insert" />). **Paint Layer 2** appears above Paint Layer 1, empty and see-through.

2. In the toolbar, right-click **Size**, type `15`, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />.

3. Set the foreground color to a dark gray: **Hue** `0`, **Saturation** `0`, **Value** `35`. Pick three spots for tufts, well apart, such as two near the top of the image and one near the bottom. At each spot, paint three **back blades**: press at the root, drag upward about a fifth of the image's height with a slight curve, and let go. Fan them out a little to the left and right. The brush ends each blade in a point.

4. Set the color to a mid gray, value `55`, and paint four **front blades** from the same roots, fanned out the same way. They cover the back blades' lower parts.

5. Set **Size** to `9` and the color to a light gray, value `72`. On two front blades of each tuft, start a third of the way up and stroke out to the tip again: the light catches the blades' ends.

	<Figure
		src={tufts}
		alt="Three gray tufts on Krita's checkered transparency pattern, each a fan of tapered blades: darker blades behind, mid-gray blades in front, and lighter tips."
		caption="Three tufts in grays, shown here with the layers below hidden. Pick the best one for your stamp."
	/>

</Steps>

## Turn a tuft into a brush

<Steps>

1. In the **Layers** docker, click the eye of **Paint Layer 1** and of **Background** to hide them. Only your tufts show, on Krita's gray and white checkers, which mean see-through. The stamp copies what you see, so the green below must be hidden.

2. Press <Kbd keys="Ctrl+R" mac="Cmd+R" /> for the **Rectangular Selection Tool** and drag a box around your best tuft, close to it but not cutting any blade.

3. Press <Kbd keys="B" />, search the Brush Presets docker for `Basic-2 Opacity`, and click it. It is a plain brush to build on.

4. Press <Kbd keys="F5" />, click **Brush Tip** in the options list, and click the **Predefined** tab. Click **Stamp** below the list of brush tips.

5. In the **Stamp** dialog, set **Name** to `Grass Tuft` and uncheck **Create mask from color**, so the tip keeps its grays. Click **Save**.

	<Figure
		src={stamp}
		alt="The Stamp dialog: a preview of the gray tuft, Name Grass Tuft, Spacing Auto, Create mask from color unchecked, Preserve alpha channel unchecked, and Style Regular."
		caption="A brush tip made from your own painting."
	/>

6. **Grass Tuft** is now the brush tip. Under **Brush mode**, choose **Lightness map**: the tip's middle gray paints the foreground color, darker grays paint darker, and lighter grays lighter. Right-click **Spacing**, type `0.6`, and press <Kbd keys="Enter" />, so the tufts print a little apart along a stroke.

	<Figure
		src={tuftTip}
		alt="The Brush Tip page on its Predefined tab: Grass Tuft selected among the brush tips, Image (181 x 126), Spacing Auto 0.60, and Brush mode Lightness map."
		caption="Lightness map: your tuft's shading in whatever color you paint with."
	/>

7. Turn each tuft with the stroke: in the options list, check the box beside **Rotation** and click its name. Check **Drawing angle** and uncheck **Pressure**.

	<Figure
		src={tuftRotation}
		alt="The Rotation option checked in the options list, with Drawing angle checked and Pressure unchecked in its inputs."
		caption="Tufts follow the direction you paint in, like grass combed by the wind."
	/>

8. Vary their size: check the box beside **Size** and click its name. Check **Fuzzy Dab**, which picks a random value for each print, and uncheck **Pressure**. Click the point at the left end of the curve, then type `50` in the percentage field to the right of the curve and press <Kbd keys="Tab" />: each tuft prints at a random size from half to full.

	<Figure
		src={tuftSize}
		alt="The Size option: Fuzzy Dab checked and Pressure unchecked, and a curve rising from 50% at the left to 100% at the right."
		caption="A random size between half and full for every tuft."
	/>

9. Spread them out: check the box beside **Scatter** and leave its settings as they are.

	<Figure
		src={tuftScatter}
		alt="The Scatter option checked, with Axis X and Axis Y and a Scatter amount curve."
		caption="Scatter moves each print a little off the stroke's line."
	/>

10. Click **Save New Brush Preset...**, name it `Grass Tuft`, and click **Save**. Press <Kbd keys="F5" /> to close the editor.

11. Choose **Select > Deselect** (<Kbd keys="Ctrl+Shift+A" mac="Cmd+Shift+A" />). In the Layers docker, click the eyes of **Paint Layer 1** and **Background** to show them again, click the eye of **Paint Layer 2** to hide your tufts, and click **Paint Layer 1** to paint on it.

</Steps>

## Stamp the grass

Paint back to front: first a dense layer of the darker green, then lighter patches on top, then a few highlights. Set **Size** in the toolbar to `110` first.

<Figure
	src={toolbarSliders}
	alt="Krita's toolbar fields: Opacity 100% and Size 110.00 px."
	caption="Size 110 px sets how big a full-size tuft prints."
/>

<Steps>

1. Set the foreground color to the **dark** swatch, `96`, `56`, `51`. Paint about twelve long, gently wandering strokes across the whole image, in different directions, and let some run off the edges. Leave a few small gaps where the shadow green shows: they read as the ground between the tufts.

	<Figure
		src={passDark}
		alt="The canvas covered in overlapping dark green tufts flowing in gently curving directions, with some dark gaps between them."
		caption="The dark pass covers the image and sets the direction of the grass."
	/>

2. Set the color to the **light** swatch, `89`, `56`, `64`. Pick four or five places for lighter patches and paint short strokes there, about a third of the image long, all around each place. Leave the rest dark.

	<Figure
		src={passLight}
		alt="The same canvas with patches of lighter green tufts on top of the dark ones."
		caption="Light patches give the ground its large shapes, which read from far away."
	/>

3. Set the color to the **highlight** swatch, `82`, `50`, `77`, and add a few short strokes in the middle of each light patch.

	<Figure
		src={passHighlight}
		alt="The canvas with a few pale yellow-green tufts in the middle of the light patches."
		caption="Highlights only in the lightest places."
	/>

</Steps>

## Check the repeat

<Steps>

1. Zoom out with <Kbd keys="Ctrl+-" mac="Cmd+-" /> until you see about three copies of the image across the canvas.

	<Figure
		src={far}
		alt="The grass texture zoomed out, repeated across the canvas: tufts and light patches join into one surface with no edges."
		caption="Zoomed out, the copies join into one surface."
	/>

2. Look at the whole. If one spot catches your eye, such as a single very light or very dark tuft, it repeats every 2 meters and gives the pattern away. Stamp over it with the **dark** green.

</Steps>

## Import it into Godot

The palette in lesson 11 needed exact colors, so it stayed lossless. Grass is different: it is big, repeated everywhere, and seen from afar, so it can use the settings Godot gives most 3D textures.

<Steps>

1. Open your project in Godot. Click `art/ground/grass_albedo.png` in the FileSystem dock and click the **Import** tab next to **Scene**.

	<Figure
		src={importBefore}
		alt="The Import dock for grass_albedo.png: Import As Texture2D, Compress Mode Lossless, Mipmaps Generate off."
		caption="The defaults: lossless and no mipmaps."
	/>

2. Set **Compress > Mode** to **VRAM Compressed**. Graphics cards can keep this kind of texture compressed in their own memory, VRAM, at a quarter of the size or less. The loss is too small to see on painted grass.

3. Under **Mipmaps**, check **Generate**. [Mipmaps](/reference/glossary/#mipmap) are the smaller copies Godot draws for ground far from the camera; without them, the tufts shimmer as the camera moves.

4. Click **Reimport (\*)**.

	<Figure
		src={importSet}
		alt="The Import dock for grass_albedo.png: Compress Mode VRAM Compressed and Mipmaps Generate checked, with the Reimport (*) button below."
		caption="VRAM Compressed with mipmaps, the usual settings for a 3D texture."
	/>

</Steps>

## Try it

- In Krita, paint a stroke with **Grass Tuft** in a bright red, then undo it. The same tufts print in any color, with your shading.
- Try the **Grass Blade** brush with **Size** `30`. Longer strokes still end in a point, because the size falls to nothing after the Fade length.
- In Godot, double-click `grass_albedo.png` in the FileSystem dock. The Inspector shows the texture and its size, 512 by 512.

## If it does not work

- **Every tuft prints as a solid green rectangle.** The layers below were visible when you clicked **Stamp**. Hide them, select the tuft again, and make the stamp again.
- **The tufts print in one flat color with no shading.** **Create mask from color** was checked in the Stamp dialog, or **Brush mode** is not **Lightness map**.
- **Blades do not taper.** **Pressure** is still checked under **Size**, or the curve rises instead of falling. Open the editor with <Kbd keys="F5" /> and check both.
- **A typed number turned into something else, or the canvas turned.** Some number keys are Krita shortcuts. Right-click a slider field before you type into it; if the canvas turned, choose **View > Zoom, Rotate, and Mirror > Reset Canvas Rotation**.
- **Tufts are all the same size.** **Size** is not checked in the options list, or **Fuzzy Dab** is not checked under it.
- **A hard line runs across the texture when you zoom out.** Wrap-around mode was off for some strokes. Stamp over the line with it on.
- **The export shows gray tufts.** Paint Layer 2 was visible. Hide it and export again.
